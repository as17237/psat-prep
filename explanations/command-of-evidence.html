PSAT 8/9 · Command of Evidence · 8 questions you missed

Does it
do the job?

Every one of these gives you a chart or a table and four statements. Here is the thing: in most of them, more than one statement is true. True is not what you are picking. You are picking the one that does a specific job — and the question always tells you what that job is.

How to use this. Read the big idea first; it is the same idea in all eight questions. Then work through the questions in order. Each one shows what you picked and why it pulled you in — that part matters more than the right answer.
The big idea

Find the job first. Then check which statement does it.

You got these eight wrong in five different ways, but four of them are the same mistake: you picked a statement that was true about the data but didn't do what the question asked for.

1

The last sentence before the blank is the job

These questions always name their job, usually right before the gap. Learn to spot the phrasing:

  • "…support the underlined conclusion" → the job is to back up that exact sentence.
  • "…support the biologists' claim" → find the claim, then back it up.
  • "…complete the student's conclusion" → whatever you pick has to follow from the data.
  • "…support the student's hypothesis" → the hypothesis is the target, not the topic.
Before looking at a single answer choice, say the job out loud in your own words. "I need something that shows the pollution came and went, not steadily." Then you are checking four statements against one clear test instead of judging which one "sounds best".
2

True is cheap. The test writers give away true statements on purpose

Look at what happened in question 4 on this page. The claim is that the condor programme has been reasonably successful. You chose: "the numbers in captivity and in the wild decreased only slightly from 2019 to 2020."

Check it against the graph and it is completely true. Wild went 338 → 330, captive went 180 → 175. Both dipped slightly. Nothing wrong with the sentence.

But now ask the job question: does "the numbers went slightly down" show that a rescue programme is succeeding? No. If anything it argues the other way.

A true statement that argues nothing, or argues the wrong way, is still wrong. Being able to verify a choice against the chart is not the same as it doing the job. That single confusion cost you four of these eight.
3

Translate the chart's words into the passage's words before you start

Test writers rarely label a chart with the same words the passage uses. In question 6 the passage talks about bonding trust and bridging trust; the graph's legend says know personally and meet for the first time. Nowhere does it tell you which is which — you have to match them from the definitions.

Do this translation in writing, on the graph itself, before reading any choice. Write "bonding" next to the right bar. Get this backwards and every choice looks equally plausible — which is exactly what happened to you.
4

Count the conditions, and check every one

When a question names two requirements, a choice has to satisfy both. Question 8 asks for a maple that is native to North America and won't grow past 60 feet. Sugar maple is native — so it passes the first test, and it is genuinely tempting. It grows to 75 feet, so it fails the second.

Underline each condition separately, then run every choice past all of them. A choice that passes the first test and fails the second is the most common wrong answer there is, because it feels right the moment you stop reading.

The four questions to ask, every time

1. What is the job? Say it in your own words before reading the choices.
2. Which words on the chart mean which words in the passage? Label them.
3. How many conditions are there? Check every choice against all of them.
4. Does this true statement actually do the job? True is not enough.

The data

Hives are mostly hexagonal cells. Worker cells are smaller than drone cells, and the bigger that size difference, the harder it is to join the sections — so bees fill the gaps with odd-shaped cells. A student consults data on three species, concluding that ______

The job: finish a conclusion that follows from the graph.

1

The passage hands you a chain of cause — write it down

This is the part that makes the question Hard. The graph alone cannot answer it. The passage gives you a chain, and you have to run the data through it:

bigger size difference → harder to join sections → more odd-shaped cells needed

Read that chain backwards and it becomes a measuring tool: fewer odd cells means a smaller size difference. Odd cells are a symptom, and you are being asked to diagnose the cause.

2

Now the graph is easy to read

Western honeybee: about 0.9% odd cells in total. The other two: about 4.9% each — more than five times as many.

Run that through the chain. Far fewer odd cells for the western honeybee means a far smaller construction problem, which means its worker and drone cells are closer in size. That is choice A, word for word.

3

Why B pulled you in

B says the bees "reserve eight-sided cells for drone eggs". It sounds technical and specific, and it does use the graph.

But go back to the first sentence of the passage: eggs are laid in hexagonal cells. The odd-shaped cells are gap filler — no eggs go in them at all. B does not just fail to do the job; it contradicts the passage.

When a choice makes a claim about something the passage already settled, that beats any data reasoning. Check the choice against the text before you check it against the chart.

The other two

C

"Many more geometrical shapes." The western honeybee uses the same three shapes as the dwarf honeybee — 5, 7 and 8-sided. Same number of shapes, far smaller amounts. C confuses how many kinds with how much.

D

Backwards. Fewer odd cells means more hexagonal cells, not fewer. If the odd shapes are only 0.9% of the hive, the hexagons must be 99.1% — the highest of the three, not the lowest.

Higher delta 15-N means more human wastewater. The tourism sites had influxes of wastewater. However, the researchers concluded that this happened intermittently.

The job: find data supporting the underlined sentence — and only that sentence.

1

Everything turns on one word

Intermittently. On and off. Starting and stopping, rather than going on steadily.

The passage already told you the tourism sites had wastewater. You are not being asked to prove that. You are being asked to prove it came and went.

So the job is: show the tourism sites going up and down, not holding steady. That is the only thing that matters. Two sites, three or four readings each.
2

Follow the two tourism rows across

Akumal Bay: 3.3, then down to 2.0, then up to 6.3.
Tulum: 5.9, then down to 2.3, then back up to 5.5.

Down, then up. Both of them. That is exactly what "on and off" looks like in numbers — and D says it plainly: values "fluctuated considerably across the three measurements from October 2016 to October 2017."

3

Why A pulled you in

A compares the tourism sites with the low-tourism sites, and everything it says is true — the tourism values generally are higher, and in February 2017 Akumal Bay (2.0) is below Mahahual (2.5) and Xahuayxol is lower still.

But comparing tourism versus non-tourism supports a different claim: that tourism sites got more wastewater. The passage already granted that. The underlined sentence is about timing, not about which site is worse.

A is a right answer to the question next door. When a choice is clearly true and clearly relevant, check which sentence it supports — not just whether it is supported.

The other two

B

Talks about missing data and the highest value in Tulum. A gap in a table shows nothing about whether pollution came and went.

C

Says values "remained relatively constant" at two of the sites — the exact opposite of intermittent. And it is false: Akumal Bay went 3.3 to 2.0.

Question 37c1e22caHard · you chose C · answer is D

"More than offset" tells you exactly which two bars to compare

The data

Modelled radial growth of sugar maples, cm per year
Climate scenarioWith nitrogenWithout nitrogen
Current0.210.16
Moderate change0.1850.15
Extreme change0.060.04

Climate change hurts growth, but the researchers concluded that nitrogen deposition could more than offset that damage — provided the change is moderate rather than extreme.

The job: show nitrogen more than making up for moderate climate damage, and failing to under extreme.

1

Turn "more than offset" into a comparison you can look up

Offsetting damage means getting back to where you started. More than offsetting means ending up better off than where you started.

So what is "where you started"? The undamaged baseline: current climate, without nitrogen — 0.16.

The claim becomes a single comparison: is with nitrogen under moderate change bigger than without nitrogen under current climate? That is 0.185 against 0.16. Yes — and only just, which is why this question is Hard.
2

Then check the second half of the claim

The researchers also said it fails under extreme change. With nitrogen under extreme change is 0.06 — far below the 0.16 baseline. Nitrogen cannot rescue that.

D states both halves: growth with nitrogen under moderate change (0.185) exceeded growth without nitrogen under the current climate (0.16), but that baseline exceeded growth with nitrogen under extreme change (0.06).

3

Why C pulled you in

C compares with-nitrogen and without-nitrogen inside moderate change: 0.185 against 0.15. True, and it does show nitrogen helping.

But helping is not the claim. The claim is that nitrogen more than cancels the damage — and you cannot show that without comparing against the undamaged baseline. C never leaves the moderate-change row, so it can only show nitrogen is better than no nitrogen. Everyone already agreed on that.

When a claim says one thing makes up for another, the comparison always crosses between the two situations. A comparison that stays inside one row cannot prove it.

The other two

A

Compares with-nitrogen current (0.21) against with-nitrogen moderate (0.185). Both have nitrogen, so this shows the damage, not the rescue.

B

Every number in B is correct — 0.16 > 0.15, and 0.15 > 0.06. But its first comparison is between two no-nitrogen bars, which measures the climate damage rather than nitrogen's rescue. A choice can be entirely true and still answer nothing.

Condors were captured in 1987 to breed them, and captive birds have been released since 1992. Many biologists claim the programme has been reasonably successful.

What would success actually look like in this table?

The whole point of the programme is to get condors back into the wild. So success means the wild row growing — and ideally overtaking the captive row, because that means the birds are surviving out there rather than only in cages.

The other two

False. Captive numbers did not steadily decrease — they dropped once from 193 to 168, then rose almost every year after.

The gap between wild and captive went from 35 in 2014 to 119 in 2017. That is not "relatively steady" — and a steady gap would not show success anyway.

Question 5ac8cfb10Hard · you chose C · answer is D

No chart at all — just find what the hypothesis needs

The question as it appeared

Gidra, a newsletter formed in 1969 by Asian American students at UCLA, raised awareness of social and political issues. Around 4,000 copies were published each month. A student hypothesises that Gidra's influence cannot be measured by the number of newsletters published monthly alone.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support the student's hypothesis?

The job: find something showing the printed count misses part of the story.

1

Read the hypothesis very literally

It is not "Gidra was influential." It is narrower and stranger: counting copies is not a good way to measure the influence.

So you need a finding that shows the number 4,000 understates the reach. Something that means one printed copy reached more than one person, or that influence flowed through a channel printing doesn't capture.
2

Test each choice against that one sentence

  • A — the founders paid for it themselves. About funding, not reach.
  • B — it also featured art and literature. About content, not reach.
  • C — it was based at UCLA's Asian American Studies Center. About location, not reach.
  • D — people passed their copies on to others after reading. Now one copy reaches two, three, five readers. The 4,000 figure counts copies, not readers — so it undercounts the influence. That is the hypothesis, exactly.
3

Why C pulled you in

C is a real fact that sounds important — an official university centre suggests weight and legitimacy. It feels like it is about influence.

But the hypothesis is not "was Gidra influential." It is "does the print count measure the influence." Where the office was tells you nothing about how many people read it.

With no chart to check against, the trap shifts: choices that are true and impressive but off-target. Keep re-reading the hypothesis word for word between choices — it is very easy to drift into answering "was it influential?" instead.

The graph never says "bonding" or "bridging"

The legend says "know personally" and "meet for the first time." The passage says "bonding" and "bridging." Nobody connects them for you — that translation is the question.

Which means the translation step is where this went wrong, not the reading of the numbers. If you had mislabelled the bars, C would look completely reasonable.

Question 8a795332aMedium · you chose B · answer is C

Two conditions means two checks

The data

Maximum height of maple trees when fully grown
Tree typeMax height (feet)Native to North AmericaPasses both?
Sugar maple75yestoo tall

Red maple60yes✓ both
Japanese maple25nonot native

A student needs a maple that is native to North America and won't grow more than 60 feet. She selects a ______

The job: find the one tree that satisfies both requirements.

1

Count the conditions before you look at the table

There are two, joined by "and":

  • Native to North America — the last column must say yes.
  • No more than 60 feet — the height must be 60 or below. "Not more than" includes exactly 60.
Two conditions means two passes through the table, not one. Cross out failures as you go and whatever survives both is your answer.
2

First pass: native. Second pass: height

Native knocks out Japanese and Norway. Three left: sugar (75), silver (70), red (60).

Height knocks out sugar and silver. Red maple is the only one standing — choice C.

3

Why B pulled you in

Sugar maple is native — so it passes the first test, and it is the most famous maple on the list. The moment it clears one requirement it feels like the answer.

But it grows to 75 feet, and the limit is 60. It fails the second test by fifteen feet.

This is the most common wrong-answer shape on the whole test: a choice that passes the first condition and fails the second. It works because most people stop checking as soon as something matches. The fix is mechanical — never accept a choice until you have run it past every condition.

The other two

A

Silver maple — same mistake as B. Native, but 70 feet.

D

Norway maple — the opposite mistake. Short enough at 50 feet, but not native. Passes the second condition and fails the first.

Your checklist for the next one of these

  • 1Say the job in your own words before reading the choices. "I need something that shows it came and went." Now you have a test rather than four opinions.
  • 2Translate the chart's labels into the passage's words. Write them on the graph. If a choice looks exactly backwards, this is usually why.
  • 3Count the conditions and check every one. Passing the first and failing the second is the most common wrong answer there is.
  • 4Name the column out loud before comparing numbers. Most tables include one or two columns that exist purely to be misread.
  • 5Then the one that cost you half of these: after checking a statement is true, ask whether it argues for the claim. True and irrelevant is still wrong — and it is the answer that feels safest, because you just verified it.
