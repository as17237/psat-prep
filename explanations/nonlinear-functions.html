Test it,
don't solve it

All three of these gave you four answers to choose from. That is a gift, and you did not use it. Every one of them can be settled by putting the choices back into the question — no algebra, no rearranging, just arithmetic you can check.

How to use this. Read the big idea first, then the three questions. Each shows what you picked and what it would have meant if it were true — that is usually the fastest way to see why it was wrong.
The big idea

Working forwards is easier than working backwards.

Solving means working backwards from an answer you don't know yet. Testing means working forwards from a number you were handed. Forwards is always easier — and on a multiple-choice test, one of the four is guaranteed to work.

1

When the choices are numbers, put them back in the question

If a question asks "what is the height?" and offers 15, 18, 30 and 36, you do not need to build an equation. Take each number, follow the words of the question, and see which one produces the value you were told.

This is not a lazy shortcut. It is often faster and safer than solving, because every step is arithmetic you can check, and you cannot make a rearranging mistake if you never rearrange.
2

When the choices are equations, test them at easy values

Pick the easiest input there is and see which equations survive. For a "how many after d days" question, the two best tests are:

  • Put in d = 0. Zero days have passed, so the equation must give back exactly what you started with.
  • Then put in d = 1. One day of change. Now you can see whether it grows, shrinks, or grows at the wrong rate.
Two quick tests usually eliminate three of the four choices. Start with d = 0 every time — it is the easiest arithmetic on the page and it catches the most wrong answers.
3

When a question asks for the peak, the zeros are the trap

For a curve that rises and falls, test writers put the places where it hits zero among the choices. Those numbers feel right because they genuinely come out of the equation — they are just answers to a different question.

The peak is halfway between the two zeros, so it is never equal to either one. If a choice turns out to be a zero, that is a signal it is wrong, not that it is right.
4

What actually happened on these three

You chose D on all three. That is not a habit worth worrying about on its own — three questions is too few to mean anything. What matters is what those three D's had in common:

  • None of them survives a ten-second test.
  • Each is what you get by doing one specific thing wrong — answering for the base instead of the height, giving the point where revenue hits zero instead of its maximum, or squaring the starting number instead of doubling it.
So the fix is mechanical, not a matter of knowing more maths: before committing to a choice, spend ten seconds putting it back into the question. All three of these would have failed that check immediately.

The habit to build

Numbers as choices? Put each one back into the question.
Equations as choices? Test at 0, then at 1.
Asked for a peak? Find the two zeros and go halfway between.

Question 10664c858Hard · you chose D (36) · answer is B (18)

Four numbers, one of which makes the area come out right

The question as it appeared

The area of a triangle is 270 square centimeters. The length of the base is 12 centimeters greater than the height. What is the height, in centimeters?

  • A15
  • B18Correct
  • C30

In plain words: which height makes a triangle with area 270?

1

Write down the two facts you were given

Area of a triangle. ½ × base × height. The half matters — forgetting it doubles every answer you get.

The base is 12 more than the height. So if the height is 15, the base is 27. If the height is 18, the base is 30. That is the only thing you need to turn a height into a full triangle.

Notice how far off your choice was. Height 36 gives an area of 864 — more than three times the 270 you were told. Any single test would have caught that.
3

Why C is the cleverer trap

C is 30. When the height is 18, the base is 30 — so 30 is a real, correct number from this triangle. It just answers a different question.

Whenever a question describes two related quantities — base and height, cost and profit, length and width — the other one is almost always among the choices. Before you commit, re-read the last line of the question and check which one it asked for.
4

The algebra, for when you want it

The quicker routeLet the height be h, so the base is h + 12 and the area is ½h(h + 12) = 270. Double both sides: h(h + 12) = 540, so h2 + 12h − 540 = 0. Factor: (h + 30)(h − 18) = 0, giving h = 18 (a length can't be −30).

Notice the 30 appearing in the factoring. That is the same 30 sitting in choice C — a number that genuinely comes out of the working, attached to the wrong thing.

The answer

B — 18 centimetres. Base 30, area ½ × 30 × 18 = 270.

3

Where 18 came from — and why it was put there

18 is not a random wrong number. It is one of the two places where this curve crosses zero. Set the revenue to nothing and factor:

−100x2 + 1,400x + 7,200 = 0

Divide everything by −100 to make it friendly: x2 − 14x − 72 = 0, which factors to (x − 18)(x + 4) = 0. So the zeros are at x = 18 and x = −4.

Now the shortcut. A parabola is a mirror, so the peak sits halfway between the two zeros: halfway between −4 and 18 is 7. The zeros are the trap; the midpoint is the answer.

And notice choice A: 4. That is the other zero with its minus sign removed — a second trap built from the same working.

4

You have met this exact trap before

On question d98c3c8c, the choices offered 55/2 and 55/4. 55/2 was where that curve came back down to zero; 55/4 was where it peaked. Same trap, different numbers.

Whenever a question asks for a maximum or a minimum, expect the zeros to appear among the choices. Finding them is useful — but only as the step before going halfway between them.

The answer

B — a $7 increase, bringing in $12,100.

C

Question 3ce6b914cMedium · you chose D · answer is B

Put in zero days, then one day

The question as it appeared

At the time of posting a video, a channel had 53 subscribers. Each day for five days, the number of subscribers doubled from the day before. Which equation gives the total subscribers, n, after d days?

  • An = (53)d
  • Bn = 53(2)dCorrect
  • Cn = 53(½)d
  • Dn = (53)2 + dYou chose this

In plain words: which of these four actually describes doubling?

1

Know what the answer must do before you look at it

Write out the first few days by hand. You are told it starts at 53 and doubles:

The real numbers. Any correct equation has to produce this row.
Days (d)0123
Subscribers53106212424
You now have a test with two easy checks: the equation must give 53 when d = 0, and 106 when d = 1.
2

First test: put in zero days

Zero days have gone by, so nothing has happened yet — the answer must be the 53 you started with. Remember that anything raised to the power of 0 equals 1.

Two choices are gone already.
ChoiceAt d = 0
A   53d530 = 1✗

C   53(½)d53 × 1 = 53✓
D   (53)2 + d2,809 + 0 = 2,809✗
Your choice, D, claims the channel had 2,809 subscribers on day zero — before anything happened. The question said 53. That is the whole check.
3

Second test: put in one day

Two choices survived. After one day the count must have doubled, to 106.

  • B: 53 × 21 = 53 × 2 = 106 ✓
  • C: 53 × ½ = 26.5 ✗ — that is halving, not doubling, and you cannot have half a subscriber.

B is the only one left. Two tests, four choices eliminated, no algebra.

4

What went wrong in D — the operations got swapped

D squares the starting number and adds the days. Doubling does neither of those things:

  • Doubling means multiplying by 2, once per day. That belongs in the base: 2d.
  • Squaring means multiplying a number by itself, once. 532 is 53 × 53 — a completely different operation, and it does not repeat.
  • Adding d means going up by one per day. That is growing by 1, not doubling.
The number that repeats goes in the base; the number of repeats goes in the exponent. Here you double, d times: 2d. The 53 is a starting amount, so it multiplies the front — it is not the thing being repeated, which is why choice A is wrong too.

The answer

B — n = 53(2)d. Starting amount out front, the thing you repeat in the base, the number of repeats in the exponent.
