PSAT 8/9 · Five questions, worked from scratch

Sketch,
don't solve

These five questions look like they need heavy algebra or a third careful read. None of them do. What they need is a picture in your head — and this page builds each picture one step at a time.

How to use this. Nothing here assumes you already know the shapes or the vocabulary. Every new word gets a box the first time it appears, and every graph is built from a table of real numbers you can check yourself. Work down the steps in order. Don't skip ahead to the answer — the answer is the least useful part.
Question 1 7ced5c84 Advanced Math · Hard

Where does a U-shape meet a flat line?

The question as it appeared

y = ax2 − c

In the equation above, a and c are positive constants. How many times does the graph of the equation above intersect the graph of the equation y = a + c in the xy-plane?

  • AZero
  • BOne
  • CTwoCorrect
  • DMore than two

In plain words: draw two graphs on the same grid. Count how many points they touch.

1

Understand the words before the maths

Positive constant. A constant is a fixed number — it doesn't change as x changes. Positive means greater than zero. So a and c are two specific numbers that nobody has told us. All we know is that both are bigger than zero.
Intersect. Cross or touch. Two graphs intersect wherever they pass through the same point.

Not knowing what a and c are feels like a problem. It isn't. The question only asks how many crossings, and that answer turns out to be the same no matter which positive numbers they are. To see the shape clearly, we'll pick easy values, then check at the end that nothing depended on our choice.

Let's use a = 1 and c = 2.

2

Build the simplest version first: y = x2

Before touching a or c, plot the plainest square there is. Pick some x values and square each one.

Squaring a negative gives a positive: (−3) × (−3) = 9.
x−3−2−10123
y = x29410149

Two things jump out of that row of numbers:

  • It's a mirror. −3 and 3 both give 9. −2 and 2 both give 4. The left half and right half are identical, because squaring cancels the minus sign.
  • It never goes below 0. The smallest value in the row is 0, at x = 0. Nothing you square can come out negative.

A graph that mirrors itself and can't go below zero has only one shape available: a valley sitting on the origin. That shape has a name.

Parabola. The U-shaped curve you get from squaring. Its turning point — the very bottom of the U, or the very top if it's upside down — is called the vertex.
3

What does the a in front do?

y = ax2 means: work out x2 first, then multiply the answer by a. So every height on the graph gets multiplied by a.

  • If a = 2, every point is twice as high — a narrower, steeper U.
  • If a = ½, every point is half as high — a wider, flatter U.
Here's the part that matters: multiplying by a positive number never changes a sign. Heights that were positive stay positive. So no positive a can flip the U upside down. It only makes it narrower or wider. That's why "a is positive" is all you need to know that it opens upward.
4

What does the − c do?

It subtracts c from every height. Every single point drops by the same amount, so the whole curve slides straight down by c. It isn't stretched or tilted — just moved.

Watch it happen with c = 2. This is the row from step 2 with 2 taken off each value:

Same shape as before, sitting 2 lower. The bottom is now at −2 instead of 0.
x−3−2−10123
y = x2 − 272−1−2−127

Still a mirror. Still a U. The vertex has moved from (0, 0) down to (0, −2).

In general, the vertex of y = ax2 − c sits at (0, −c). And since c is positive, −c is negative — so the bottom of the U is always below the x-axis. You never needed a formula for that; it came straight from the table.
5

Now the second graph: why y = a + c is a flat line

Look closely at y = a + c. There is no x in it anywhere. That's the whole trick.

a and c are fixed numbers, so a + c is just some fixed number too. With our values it's 1 + 2 = 3. Since x never appears, moving along the x-axis changes nothing — y is 3 when x is 0, when x is 100, when x is −50.

No x in the equation means a horizontal line. The height is always the same, so the graph is flat.

Is that line above or below the x-axis? a is positive and c is positive, and a positive plus a positive is always positive. So a + c is above the axis — always, whatever the actual numbers are.

6

Put both on one grid and count

You now know two facts, and they're all you need:

  • The U opens upward, and its lowest point is below the axis.
  • The line is flat, and it sits above the axis.

So the line is above the bottom of the U. Now follow the curve with your finger: start at the bottom of the U, below the line. Travel right — the U climbs, and it climbs forever, so at some point it must pass through the line. That's one crossing. Go back to the bottom and travel left instead — same thing, because the U is a mirror image. That's the second.

7

Check that our made-up numbers didn't matter

We picked a = 1, c = 2. Would anything change with different positive numbers? Re-read the two facts from step 6:

  • "Opens upward" came from a being positive — true for every positive a.
  • "Vertex below the axis" came from c being positive — true for every positive c.
  • "Line above the axis" came from positive + positive being positive — always true.

Nothing leaned on the specific values. The picture is the same every time, so the count is too.

The answer

C — Two.

An upward U whose bottom is below a flat line has to come up through that line on both sides.

Why the other three are wrong

A

Zero would need the line to be below the bottom of the U, so they never meet. But the bottom is below the axis and the line is above it, so the line is definitely higher.

B

One happens in exactly one situation: the line touches the U precisely at its vertex, grazing it. That would need the line's height to equal −c, but the line's height is a + c — a positive number, and −c is negative. They can't be equal.

D

More than two is impossible for any straight line and any parabola, no matter the numbers. Once the U has turned around and started climbing, it never comes back down to try again. Worth memorising: a straight line can cut a parabola at most twice.

Question 2 1224cb23 Advanced Math · Hard

A curve that only ever climbs

The question as it appeared

y = 9(a7)x+c − b

How many times does the graph of the given equation in the xy-plane cross the x-axis, where a, b, and c are positive constants such that a > 7 and b > c?

  • AZero
  • BOneCorrect
  • CTwo
  • DThree

In plain words: this curve starts low and ends high. How many times does it pass through height zero on the way?

1

"Cross the x-axis" just means "reach height zero"

The x-axis is the line where y = 0. So the question is: how many times does this curve pass through height 0?

That is worth saying out loud, because it means you only need three things: where the curve starts, where it ends, and whether it ever turns around. You never have to work out where it crosses.

2

Pick friendly numbers so you can see what the curve does

a, b and c are unknown, so choose values that obey every rule given and make the arithmetic easy. The rules are: all three positive, a > 7, and b > c.

Take a = 14, b = 36, c = 1. All positive, 14 is bigger than 7, and 36 is bigger than 1. The equation becomes:

y = 9 × 2x+1 − 36
The useful part is a/7 = 14/7 = 2. A base of 2 means the curve doubles — which is the easiest kind of growth to picture.
3

An x in the exponent means "keep doubling"

Exponent. The small raised number saying how many times to multiply. 23 means 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. The 2 is the base; the 3 is the exponent.

Normally the exponent is a fixed number. Here it is x + 1, so it changes as you move along the graph. Every time x goes up by 1, the exponent goes up by 1 — which means one more multiplication by 2.

Stepping right doubles the value. It doesn't add a fixed amount — it multiplies. That is what makes the curve bend upward instead of staying straight, and it means the curve only ever goes up.
4

Going left, it halves — and halving never reaches zero

If stepping right doubles, stepping left halves. Keep halving and the number shrinks: 36, 18, 9, 4.5, 2.25 …

But halving a positive number can never reach zero, and can never turn it negative. It gets tinier and tinier and stays above zero forever.

So the 9 × 2x+1 part is always positive: nearly nothing on the far left, enormous on the right.

5

The − 36 drops the whole curve, and gives it a floor

Same rule as question 1: subtracting moves every point down by that amount. The rising shape is unchanged; it just sits 36 lower.

Before, the curve's lowest possible level was 0 — approached but never reached. Now that level is −36. Watch it in the numbers:

Each step right doubles the first part, so 9 → 18 → 36 → 72 → 144, and 36 comes off every one.
x−10123
y−27−18036108
Because b is positive, the floor −b is below zero. So the curve starts below the axis — and it climbs from there without ever stopping.
6

Count the crossings

Three facts, and the answer falls straight out:

  • It starts below zero — its floor is −b.
  • It ends above zero — it doubles forever.
  • It only ever goes up, so it never turns around.

To get from below zero to above zero, it must pass through zero. And because it never comes back down, it can never cross a second time. Exactly one.

7

Check the friendly numbers didn't decide it

You picked 14, 36 and 1. Would other values change the answer? Look at what each fact actually rested on:

  • "It doubles" came from a > 7, which makes a/7 bigger than 1. Any such a gives growth — maybe slower than doubling, but always upward.
  • "The floor is below zero" came from b being positive. True for every positive b.

Neither leaned on your specific choices, so the count is one no matter what the real values are.

8

Notice the fact you never used

The question told you b > c. Look back through every step — it appears nowhere. You needed b positive, and you needed a > 7. How b compares to c never mattered.

Hard questions often hand you a fact you don't need, so that you'll hunt for a use and lose time. Finishing with a leftover fact is normal. It is not a sign you did something wrong.

The answer

B — One.

A curve that only climbs can pass any given height at most once. This one starts below zero and ends above it, so it passes exactly once.

Why the other three are wrong

A

Zero would mean the curve never reaches height 0 — it would have to stay entirely below the axis, or entirely above it. But the table shows it at −27 and later at +108, so it is on both sides of zero. It has to cross somewhere in between.

C

Two and D — Three both need the curve to turn around. To cross zero a second time it would have to come back down. A curve that multiplies by something bigger than 1 at every step never does. Both can be eliminated the moment you notice the base is above 1 — before any other work.

·

These two questions are secretly the same question. Both ask "how many crossings", and neither wants you to find where. You sketch the shape, note where it sits relative to the line, and count. That's the single most useful habit on this page.

Question 3 d98c3c8c Advanced Math · Hard

Lift a hill by 3 and the summit rises by 3

The question as it appeared

f(t) = 55t − 2t2

The function f is defined by the given equation. The function g is defined by g(t) = f(t) + 3. Which expression represents the maximum value of g(t)?

  • A3 + (55/2)2
  • B3 + 2(55/4)2Correct
  • C3 − 2(55/4)2
  • D3 − (55/2)2

In plain words: find the highest point of one curve, then add 3.

1

Read what the two lines actually say

Function. A machine with one input and one output. f(t) is read "f of t" and means: put t in, get a number out. It is not f times t.

The first line is a recipe. Put in t = 10 and you get 55(10) − 2(100) = 350.

The second line says g(t) = f(t) + 3: run the f machine, then add 3. That's all g ever does.

2

So you never have to think about g again

If g is always exactly 3 more than f, then g's highest point is exactly 3 more than f's highest point.

The whole question is now: what is the highest value of f? Add 3 to it. Two jobs, in that order.
3

Job one: check that f is a hill, so it has a highest point

Put a few values in and see what comes out.

Up, then down. That's a hill, so there is a top to find.
t010152027.5
f(t)03503753000

In question 1 the squared term was positive and gave an upward U. Here the squared term is negative — that's the − 2t2 — which flips the U upside down into a hill.

The table shows the top is somewhere near 15. Now pin it down exactly.

4

Find where the top is

A parabola is a mirror, so the top sits exactly halfway between the two places it touches zero. Find those two places by factoring — both terms share a t:

55t − 2t2 = t(55 − 2t)

A multiplication is zero only when one of its parts is zero. So either t = 0, or 55 − 2t = 0, which gives t = 27.5. Both match the table above.

Halfway between 0 and 27.5:

t = 27.5 ÷ 2 = 13.75
Written as a fraction, 13.75 = 55/4 — and that is the form the answer choices use, so keep it in mind. Note also that 27.5 = 55/2: that's where the hill comes back down to zero, not where the top is. Two of the wrong answers are built from mixing these two up.
5

Job one, finished: how high is the top?

Put t = 13.75 into the recipe. Two pieces to work out:

55 × 13.75 = 756.25
2 × 13.752 = 378.125

Then subtract the second from the first:

756.25 − 378.125 = 378.125
That's not a typoThe number you take away and the number you're left with really are the same. That happens because 378.125 is exactly half of 756.25 — and taking half of something away leaves the other half. It's a coincidence of this particular question, not a rule, so don't go looking for it elsewhere.

So the highest value of f is 378.125.

6

Job two: add 3, then find that number among the choices

378.125 + 3 = 381.125

The choices are written as expressions rather than plain numbers, so work each one out and look for 381.125. This is the reliable move whenever answers are written as expressions — comparing numbers is much safer than comparing symbols.

Only B matches.
ChoiceWorks out to
A   3 + (55/2)2759.25
B   3 + 2(55/4)2381.125
C   3 − 2(55/4)2−375.125
D   3 − (55/2)2−753.25

The answer

B — 3 + 2(55/4)2, which equals 381.125.

Why the other three are wrong

A

Uses 55/2 instead of 55/4 — the place where the curve hits zero, not where it peaks. This is the single most common slip on this question.

C

Right number, wrong sign: subtracts instead of adding.

D

Both mistakes at once — wrong t value and wrong sign.

!

A shortcut worth having. C and D are both negative. But the table in step 3 shows f reaching 375, and g is even higher. A curve that climbs to 375 cannot have a negative maximum. You could have crossed out C and D in seconds, leaving a coin flip between A and B before doing any real work.

Question 4 fde9312b Craft and Structure · Hard

What job is this phrase doing?

The question as it appeared

In response to concerns that some recent financial crises were exacerbated by consumers misunderstanding risks associated with credit cards, loans, and other financial products, policymakers in many countries have instituted risk-disclosure requirements on sellers of those products. Enrique Seira et al. investigated a variety of risk-disclosure messages sent to thousands of credit card customers and found that the messages had only small and short-lived effects on behavior. Seira et al. asserted that such effects may nevertheless be worth pursuing, given the negligible cost of messaging.

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined portion in the text as a whole?

  • AIt notes a factor that led Seira et al. to not dismiss risk-disclosure messaging altogether despite their evidence of its limited utility.Correct
  • BIt acknowledges a type of risk-disclosure messaging that Seira et al. may not have fully accounted for in their study.
  • CIt describes a consideration that explains why Seira et al. recommended risk-disclosure messaging even though its effects may be small relative to its costs.
  • DIt points out a circumstance that Seira et al. conceded may make risk-disclosure messaging more effective than their study suggests.
1

"Function" here has nothing to do with maths

Function (in reading). The job a sentence does for the argument around it. Not what it means — what it's there for.

Compare: "It was raining, so I took an umbrella." The meaning of the underlined half is that you took an umbrella. Its function is to state the result of the rain. The question is always asking the second kind of thing.

2

Translate the hard words first

This passage is dense on purpose. Swap in plain words:

Exacerbated — made worse.
Instituted — put in place, set up.
Risk-disclosure requirements — rules forcing companies to warn you about the risks.
et al. — "and others". Seira and his fellow researchers.
Asserted — claimed, stated.
Nevertheless — even so; despite what was just said.
Negligible — so small it hardly counts. Nearly nothing.
Utility — usefulness.
Dismiss — throw out, give up on.
3

Retell the passage as a short story

With plain words, the whole thing is four beats:

  • People got confused about credit card risks, and that made financial crises worse.
  • So governments made companies send warning messages.
  • Researchers checked whether the warnings worked. They barely did — small effects that faded fast.
  • But the researchers said: keep doing it anyway, because the messages cost almost nothing.

The underlined phrase is that last beat — the "because" at the end.

4

Let the connector words tell you the job

Two small words in the final sentence do all the signposting. Most students read straight past them.

The finding
…the messages had only small and short-lived effects.
Bad news for the messages. This is what the study actually found.
The pivot
…may nevertheless be worth pursuing…
Nevertheless means "despite what I just said." They're keeping the idea alive in spite of their own weak result. So whatever comes next has to be their reason for not giving up.
The reason
given the negligible cost of messaging
Given introduces a reason — it's a formal way of saying "because of". The cost is almost nothing, and when something costs almost nothing, even a tiny benefit is worth having.
So the job of the underlined phrase is: to give the reason they didn't give up on the idea, even though their own evidence was weak.
5

Say it yourself before reading the choices

This is the habit that makes these questions much easier. Finish this sentence out loud before looking at A, B, C, D:

"The underlined part is here to …" → "…explain why they still think it's worth doing even though it barely worked."

Now go find the choice that says that. A says almost exactly it: a factor that led them to not dismiss it altogether despite evidence of its limited utility. Match.

If you read the four choices first, all of them sound plausible — they're written to. Deciding what you're looking for before you look is what stops that.

The answer

A. The underlined phrase gives the reason the researchers didn't abandon the idea despite weak results.

Why the other three are wrong

C

The dangerous one. It uses every word the right answer uses — small effects, costs, a consideration. But read what it actually claims: effects are small relative to its costs. That's backwards. The passage says the costs are tiny compared to the effects, which is exactly why it's still worth doing. C describes a bad deal; the passage describes a cheap bet. When two choices use the same words, check which way round the comparison runs.

B

Calls the phrase a type of messaging. It isn't a type of anything — it's a statement about cost. Nothing in the passage mentions different kinds of messages being missed.

D

Says the phrase makes messaging more effective. It doesn't touch effectiveness — effectiveness is still low and nobody disputes that. What the phrase changes is whether low effectiveness is acceptable. Cheap and barely-works is still worth it; that's not the same as working better.

Question 5 e465d17e Command of Evidence · Hard

Real numbers, wrong story

The question as it appeared

Mean ratings after 21 days · higher = greater well-being
MeasureAware of taking a placeboControl groupWhich is higher?
Global improvement5.03.9aware ↑
Symptom severity reduction92.0046.00aware ↑
Quality of life improvement11.45.4aware ↑

To test whether a medication is effective, scientists compare outcomes for patients taking it and patients taking a placebo (a medically inactive substance). Patients normally aren't told they're receiving a placebo, but a research team conducted a study to see if there might be a medical benefit to telling them. The team used various measures to evaluate participants, with higher ratings indicating greater well-being in each measure. Compared to the mean ratings after 21 days for participants in the control group, the mean ratings for participants who were aware of taking a placebo ______

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to complete the statement?

  • Aranged from 5.0 to 92.00, indicating that well-being varied widely from participant to participant.
  • Bwere lower for two measures, with the rating for only one measure indicating greater well-being for these participants.
  • Cranged from 3.9 to 46.00, with no rating indicating greater well-being in any measure for these participants.
  • Dwere higher for all three measures, indicating greater overall well-being for these participants.Correct
1

Understand the setup before the numbers

Placebo. A fake treatment — a sugar pill — with no medicine in it.
Control group. The group used as a baseline for comparison. Here: the people who were not told they were taking a placebo.
Mean. The average. Add all the values and divide by how many there are.

The study asks an odd question: if we tell people their pill is fake, do they still do better? So there are two groups — those who knew, and the control group.

One line in the passage decides the whole question: higher ratings mean greater well-being. Without it you couldn't tell whether a bigger number was good or bad news. Always hunt for that sentence in a table question.
2

Read the table's structure before its contents

Check what the rows are and what each column is, so you don't compare the wrong things.

  • Each row is a different measure — a different way of scoring well-being.
  • The middle column is the group who knew they were on a placebo.
  • The right column is the control group.

The sentence you're completing is about the group who knew. So the middle column is your subject, and the right column is what you're comparing it against.

3

Compare one row at a time

Look back at the table at the top of this question — everything you need is in it.

Row by row: 5.0 beats 3.9. 92.00 beats 46.00. 11.4 beats 5.4. The aware group is higher in every row — three out of three.

Now write your own one-line summary before reading the choices: "the aware group scored higher on all three measures." Same habit as question 4 — decide what you're looking for, then look.
4

Test each choice against the table

Every wrong choice here contains numbers that genuinely appear in the table. Being real is not the test. The test is whether the sentence built around those numbers is true. Check them one at a time:

  • A — 5.0 and 92.00 are real. But it claims well-being "varied widely from participant to participant." Look at what the table gives you: means. One average per group. An average tells you nothing about how much individual people differed from each other. This claim isn't supported by anything in the table.
  • B — says two measures were lower. Count them: zero were lower. Straightforwardly false.
  • C — 3.9 and 46.00 are real numbers, but they come from the control column. The sentence is about the aware group. Wrong column entirely.
  • D — higher for all three measures. That's exactly what we found in step 3.

The answer

D. The aware group's mean rating was higher on all three measures, and since higher means better, that indicates greater overall well-being.

The three ways a table choice goes wrong

A

A claim the data can't support. Averages can't tell you about variation between individuals. If a choice mentions spread, range between people, or consistency, check whether you were actually given that — or just means.

B

Simply false. Checkable in seconds by counting rows. Always count.

C

Right numbers, wrong column. Before accepting any choice, confirm which column its numbers came from and which group the sentence is about.

  • 2"How many times?" is a counting question, not a solving question. Sketch both shapes, see where they sit relative to each other, count. A straight line meets a parabola at most twice. A curve that only ever climbs meets any height at most once.
  • 3When the choices are expressions, turn them all into numbers. Work out your own answer as a number first, then evaluate all four and match. And strike out any choice that's the wrong sign before you start.
  • 4For reading questions, answer in your own words before looking at the choices. Say "this phrase is here to ___" out loud. The connector words — nevertheless, given, despite, because — tell you the job.
  • 5Real numbers don't make a choice right. Check which column they came from, check every row, and check the claim wrapped around them is something the data can actually show.
